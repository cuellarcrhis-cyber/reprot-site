Exercise Snacks: Why One-Minute Workouts Count

October 29, 2026 · 5 min read

You've been meaning to "start working out" since March.

The plan is always the same: a real routine, 45 minutes, four days a week, starting Monday. Monday comes. You're tired. The plan needs a gym bag, a free hour and a good mood, and you have none of them.

Meanwhile you had a free minute 40 times today. You spent most of them on your phone.

Here's the good news: those minutes count. Short bursts of real effort, done a few times a day, have measurable effects on fitness. Researchers call them exercise snacks, and they might be the easiest way to stop putting it off.

What are exercise snacks?

An exercise snack is a short bout of vigorous movement, usually one minute or less of hard work, repeated a few times across the day with hours in between. Think climbing three flights of stairs fast, 15 bodyweight squats, or a minute of jumping jacks.

The key word is vigorous. A slow walk to the fridge is movement, which is good, but it isn't a snack. A snack should leave you breathing hard enough that talking feels awkward for a few seconds.

What the research actually shows

This is a young area of research, so it's worth being precise about what we know.

Short stair bursts improved fitness

In a 2019 study from McMaster University, sedentary young adults climbed three flights of stairs as fast as they could, three times a day, with one to four hours between bouts, three days a week for six weeks. Compared with a group that didn't train, the stair climbers improved their peak oxygen uptake (a standard measure of cardio fitness), by roughly 5%. They also produced more power on a cycling test.

The caveats: it was small (12 people per group), short, and the gain was modest. But it showed that a few minutes of total effort per day, broken into tiny pieces, did something measurable.

Everyday bursts were linked to lower risk of death

A 2022 study in Nature Medicine used wrist-worn activity trackers on more than 25,000 UK adults who said they didn't exercise. Researchers looked for short bursts of vigorous activity in daily life, like hurrying up stairs or walking fast to catch a bus, which they called VILPA (vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity).

Over about seven years of follow-up, people who did around three bursts a day of one to two minutes each had a 38% to 40% lower risk of death from any cause and from cancer, and a 48% to 49% lower risk of death from cardiovascular disease, compared with people who did none.

Important: this was an observational study. It shows a link, not proof that the bursts caused the lower risk. People who move more briskly may differ in other ways. Still, it's a striking result for a few minutes a day.

Official guidelines dropped the 10-minute rule

For years, public health advice said activity only "counted" in bouts of ten minutes or more. The 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans removed that requirement: any amount of moderate-to-vigorous activity counts toward your weekly total. The WHO's 2020 guidelines say the same thing in four words: every move counts.

Exercise snacks you can do anywhere

You don't need stairs or a gym. Here are options that work in a bedroom, an office or a kitchen, with an easier version of each.

SnackOne roundEasier version
Bodyweight squats15–20 at a steady, brisk paceSquat to a chair and stand back up
Push-upsAs many good reps as you can, up to 15Hands on a counter or the edge of a bed
Plank20–40 seconds, body in a straight lineKnees down, or hands on a bench
Stair climb2–3 flights, fast but in controlOne flight, walking briskly
Sit-ups10–15 controlled repsCrunches, or partial range

Squats and stairs are the best for getting your heart rate up. Push-ups and planks build strength more than cardio. A mix across the day gives you some of both.

How to start: a one-week plan

The hard part of exercise snacks isn't the exercise. It's remembering to do them. So tie each one to something that already happens every day.

  1. Pick three anchors. Good ones: right after your alarm, after lunch, the first time you open a social app in the evening.
  2. Pick one exercise per anchor. Keep it boring. Squats in the morning, push-ups after lunch, a plank in the evening.
  3. Set a tiny number. Ten reps or 20 seconds. Small enough that you can't argue with it.
  4. Do it for seven days. Don't add more until you've hit all three anchors most days.
  5. Then turn it up. More reps, a harder version, or a faster pace. The goal is a few bursts a day where you're genuinely working.

Who should be careful

Short bursts are safe for most healthy people, but "vigorous" is relative. If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, joint problems, are pregnant, or haven't been active in a long time, check with a doctor first and start with the easier versions. Stop if anything hurts in a sharp or unusual way; muscle burn is fine, joint pain isn't.

TL;DR

  • Exercise snacks are short bursts of hard effort, about a minute, a few times a day.
  • A small 2019 trial found three daily stair sprints improved cardio fitness by about 5% in six weeks.
  • A large 2022 observational study linked three 1–2 minute daily bursts to substantially lower risk of death.
  • US and WHO guidelines say every bit of moderate-to-vigorous activity counts, not just 10-minute bouts.
  • Tie each snack to a daily anchor and start tiny: ten reps or 20 seconds.
