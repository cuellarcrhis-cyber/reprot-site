How Long Does It Take to Refocus After a Notification?

How long does it take to refocus after a notification? What the famous 23-minute number really measured, what's unproven, and how to get back faster.

Crhistian C·Oct 31, 2026·9 min read

TL;DR

  • There's no reliable single number for how long it takes to refocus after a notification.
  • The famous "23 minutes" (about 25 in the published paper) is the average time before people returned to an interrupted task, including other work in between, and it varied hugely.
  • Even an ignored notification raised errors in a lab study, and switching leaves "attention residue" on the unfinished task.
  • "15 minutes to flow" is a 1987 book's estimate; "beta to alpha brainwaves" isn't established.
  • Best moves: write a one-line resume note before switching, batch notifications with Scheduled Summary, use a Focus, and keep the phone out of reach.
  • About half of interruptions are self-interruptions, so you need a plan for the urge, not just the ping.

You're three paragraphs into the hard part of a report. The sentence you need is almost there.

Your phone lights up on the desk. A group chat. You don't even open it. You just read the preview, think "later," and look back at the screen.

The sentence is gone. You reread your last paragraph. Then the one before it. Somehow it's 10:40.

So how long does it take to refocus after a notification? You've probably seen the answer on a slide or a reel: 23 minutes. It's one of the most repeated numbers in productivity. It's also one of the most misunderstood, and the truth is more useful than the myth.

How long does it take to refocus after a notification? The short answer

Nobody has a reliable single number, and the famous one measures something else.

The "23 minutes and 15 seconds" comes from Gloria Mark, an informatics professor at UC Irvine who has studied workplace attention for about two decades. In an interview with Gallup, Mark described tracking interrupted work: most of it was picked back up the same day, after an average of 23 minutes and 15 seconds. Her peer-reviewed 2005 paper with Victor González and Justin Harris, from observing information workers at their desks, reports a similar figure: about 25 minutes before people returned to an interrupted task.

~25 minutesAverage time before people went back to an interrupted task in Mark, González and Harris (2005). Not the time it took to refocus.

Here's the part that usually gets dropped. That number is the time until people went back to the task, not the time it took their brain to reboot. In between, people typically worked on about two other things first. And the spread was enormous: the standard deviation was nearly 55 minutes, so some people returned in a minute and others hours later.

So the honest answer is: an interruption often costs you far more than the few seconds it takes to glance at it, because it tends to pull you into other work before you return. How long it costs you depends mostly on what happens right after the ping.

Even a notification you ignore costs something

You might think the fix is just not opening it. Unfortunately, the ping itself does damage.

In a 2015 study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, Cary Stothart and colleagues had college students do a dull, attention-heavy computer task. Partway through, some of them got a call or text notification on their own phone, sent by the researchers without warning. They didn't answer. They still made more errors than students who got no notification, and calls were worse than texts. The authors' explanation: the alert sparks thoughts unrelated to the task, and your mind wanders off after them.

Caveats: it was a lab task with students, not real work, and it measured mistakes in the minutes right after, not how long focus took to come back. But it's a clean result. The buzz alone is enough to pull part of you away.

There's a name for the part of you that stays behind. The management researcher Sophie Leroy calls it attention residue: when you switch away from an unfinished task, some of your attention stays stuck on it, and you do the next thing worse. Your brain doesn't close tabs cleanly.

What's known and what isn't

A lot of focus content goes past the evidence. Here's where the popular claims stand.

ClaimWhat the evidence says
"It takes 23 minutes to refocus."Shaky as stated. It's the average time before people returned to an interrupted task, including other work in between, with huge variation.
"A notification you ignore still hurts focus."Supported in a lab study (Stothart, 2015). Real-world size of the effect is unclear.
"It takes 15 minutes to get into flow."An estimate from Peopleware, a 1987 book on software teams. A reasonable rule of thumb, not a measured number.
"Focus means your brain shifts from beta to alpha waves."Not established. A 2022 review of 25 brain studies on flow found the results inconsistent; no single brainwave pattern reliably marks focus.
"Interruptions make you slower."Not always. In Mark's 2008 experiment, interrupted people finished as fast or faster with similar quality, but reported more stress, frustration and time pressure.

That last one matters. Interruptions often don't show up as worse work. They show up as you working faster and feeling worse, which is its own cost by 5 p.m.

Half your interruptions don't come from your phone

Mark has also said that about half the time, we interrupt ourselves. No buzz, no banner. You hit a hard sentence and your hand just goes for the phone.

That changes the problem. Notifications train the habit, but once it's trained, the itch shows up on its own. In Mark's newer work, the average time on one screen before switching dropped to about 47 seconds (we dug into that in The Phone Is the Enemy of Focus). Turning notifications off handles the outside half. You need something for the inside half too.

And that urge isn't a character flaw. It's usually a small escape from something difficult, delivered by apps built to be the easiest escape in the room. We wrote about why in Willpower Isn't the Problem.

How to refocus after a notification, faster

You can't make interruptions free. You can make them cheaper and rarer. Five moves, in order of payoff:

  1. Leave yourself a 30-second note before you switch. When something pulls you away, write one line: where you stopped and what's next. ("Finish the pricing paragraph, then the chart.") In a 2018 study by Sophie Leroy and Theresa Glomb, people who wrote a quick "ready-to-resume" plan before switching showed less attention residue and did better on the next task. It's the cheapest fix there is.
  2. Batch notifications into a few deliveries a day. On iPhone, go to Settings → Notifications → Scheduled Summary, turn it on, pick times (say 12:00 and 6:00), and choose which apps go in it. Messages from people can stay immediate.
  3. Turn on a Focus for deep work. In Settings → Focus, set up a Work or Do Not Disturb focus and allow only the people and apps you'd actually stop for. Silenced notifications wait for you instead of interrupting.
  4. Put the phone out of reach. Face-down still lights up and still invites the self-interruption. Another room turns "just a second" into a decision.
  5. When you get pulled away, come back physically. Stand up, take a breath, reread your note, then sit back down. A short reset beats rereading the same paragraph three times.
3 times a dayPeople whose notifications arrived in three daily batches felt more focused and in control than those who got them as usual (Fitz et al., 2019).

About that batching study: it was a randomized field experiment with 237 smartphone users in India over two weeks. People who got notifications in three daily batches reported feeling more attentive, more productive and more in control of their phones. People who got no notifications at all felt more anxious and more worried about missing out. So the goal isn't silence. It's fewer, predictable interruptions on your schedule.

A privacy note: Scheduled Summary and Focus are built into iOS, so you don't need to give a third-party app access to your notifications to use them.

Is it bad to check notifications while working?

Not morally, and not always. A message from your kid's school or your manager may be exactly what you should stop for. The cost comes from treating every ping as equally urgent, all day.

A simple test: if a notification would still matter in an hour, it can wait for the summary. If it wouldn't matter in an hour, it didn't need to reach you at all. For the apps where "checking" becomes 40 minutes of scrolling, set limits too; How to Stop Doomscrolling on iPhone walks through the settings.

If you try the plank reset: forearms under your shoulders, body in a straight line from head to heels, squeeze your glutes, breathe normally. Easier version: knees down, or hands on the edge of your desk. Stop if you feel pain, and check with a doctor first if you have a back or shoulder injury, are pregnant, have a heart condition or haven't exercised in a long time. A few slow squats, or just standing and stretching, work too. Small movement breaks count; we explained why in Exercise Snacks.
