How to Check Screen Time on iPhone (and What the Numbers Mean)

How to check screen time on iPhone in iOS 27 and iOS 26, what pickups and notifications really count, and a 7-day way to read your report honestly.

Crhistian C·Nov 15, 2026·9 min read

TL;DR

  • In iOS 27, check screen time on iPhone in Settings → Screen Time → Daily Average, then tap Week or Day. On iOS 26: See All App & Website Activity.
  • Scroll down for Pickups (how often you wake the phone) and Notifications (how many you received). Neither is time.
  • With Share Across Devices on, your totals can include your iPad and Mac.
  • Your guess is probably wrong: in one study of 325 iPhone users, estimates were off by about 19 hours a week on average.
  • There's no official "too much" number for adults. Ask what it's pushing out and when it happens.
  • Use one week of data, pick one change, and compare next Sunday.

Someone asks how much you're on your phone. You say "maybe two hours, tops." It sounds about right. You've never actually looked.

Then Sunday morning the Weekly Report slides down from the top of the screen. You tap it. The number has a 5 in front of it.

That gap between the guess and the log is normal, and it's the whole reason to check. Here's how to check screen time on iPhone in iOS 27 (and iOS 26), what each number actually counts, and a simple week-long way to read your report without spiraling into guilt.

How to check screen time on iPhone (iOS 27)

The short version: Settings → Screen Time → Daily Average.

  1. Open the Settings app.
  2. Tap Screen Time, then tap Daily Average.
  3. Tap Week for a summary of the week, or Day for a single day.
  4. Scroll down to see your Pickups and Notifications.
  5. Swipe left and right on the summary to look back through earlier days and weeks.

If you see no data, Screen Time is probably off. In iOS 27, go to Settings → Screen Time → Manage Screen Time and tap Turn On App & Website Activity. It only records from that moment on, so give it a full week before you judge anything.

Two faster ways in: tap the Screen Time Weekly Report notification when it shows up (it stays in Notification Center if you miss it), or add the Screen Time widget to your Home Screen so the day's total is always one glance away.

On iOS 26 or earlier

The menu names are older. Go to Settings → Screen Time → See All App & Website Activity, then pick Week or Day. Tap Devices at the top if you want to see one device at a time. Activity that doesn't fit a category shows up in gray in the chart.

What the numbers on your Screen Time report mean

The report shows four kinds of numbers. They measure different things, and mixing them up is where most people misread their week.

NumberWhat it countsWhat it tells you
Daily average / totalTime spent in apps and websitesHow big the problem is overall
Most used apps and categoriesThat time, split by app or type (social, entertainment, games)Where the time actually goes
PickupsHow many times you woke the phoneHow often you reach for it
NotificationsHow many notifications you received, by appWho's pulling you back in

Apple's own definitions are short: pickups are the number of times you wake a device, and notifications are the number of notifications received from apps. Neither one is time. You can have 40 pickups and an hour of use, or 15 pickups and five hours.

Your total might include your iPad and Mac

This one catches people. In iOS 27, if Share Across Devices is on (Settings → Screen Time → Manage Screen Time), your Screen Time reports and usage are shared across your iPhone, iPad and Mac on the same Apple Account, and you can see combined activity. A long workday on your laptop can make your "phone" number look worse than it is.

That's not a bug. It's often the more honest number. Just know which one you're looking at before you decide your phone is the villain.

What your pickups and notifications are telling you

The total gets all the attention. Pickups and notifications are usually more useful, because they show the pattern behind it.

Read them together:

  • Lots of pickups, short sessions: checking on reflex. The fix is fewer triggers: fewer notifications, and a phone that's not in your hand's reach.
  • Few pickups, long sessions: you get pulled in and stay. The fix is a stop point: a limit or schedule on the app that holds you.
  • One app tops your notifications: that app is setting your schedule. Turn off its notifications or move it into a summary.

Your pickup count can also rise without much real use. With Raise to Wake on, lifting the phone to check the time wakes the screen. If you want fewer accidental wake-ups, it's in Settings → Display & Brightness.

For the notifications that can wait, Apple's Scheduled Summary bundles them into a few deliveries a day: Settings → Notifications → Scheduled Summary, set your times, then choose the apps. That's close to what one trial tested. In a two-week randomized experiment with 237 people in India, those whose notifications arrived in batches three times a day reported feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control of their phones than people getting alerts as usual. People who got no notifications at all felt more anxious, not less. Some of the authors had ties to a related app, so read it as one promising trial, not the final word.

Why every ping costs more than the second it takes is the subject of How Long Does It Take to Refocus After a Notification?

Is iPhone screen time accurate? More than your guess

Screen Time is a log, not a perfect one. Counts can include other devices, some activity lands in no category, and like any tracker it can glitch. But it's far closer to the truth than what most of us would say off the top of our heads.

In a 2020 study of 325 iPhone users, researchers compared people's estimates of their weekly use with their Screen Time numbers. On average, estimates of overall iPhone use were off by about 19 hours a week, and social media estimates by about 12 hours. People who reported more depressive symptoms, and people who used their phones more, tended to misjudge by more. It's a one-time snapshot of one group, not a trial, but the pattern matches the wider research.

~19 hours/weekAverage gap between people's guess and their iPhone's Screen Time log, in a study of 325 iPhone users (Sewall et al., 2020).

A 2021 meta-analysis pooled 106 comparisons of self-reported versus logged digital media use and found the two were only moderately related; self-reports were rarely an accurate reflection of the logs.

So the point of checking isn't to catch yourself out. It's that you can't fix a number you're guessing at.

How much screen time is too much?

There's no official daily number for adults, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. Two hours of messaging your family and two hours of a feed you don't even enjoy are not the same two hours.

A better test is three questions about your own report:

  1. Is it pushing out something you care about? Sleep, moving your body, people, the thing you keep saying you'll start.
  2. Is it when you'd choose? An hour at lunch is different from an hour in bed at 1 a.m.
  3. Do you feel better or worse after? Your top app is a good place to ask this honestly.

If phone use is tangled up with feeling low, anxious or stuck, a number on a chart isn't the fix. Talking to a doctor or a mental health professional is a good next step, and if you're in crisis in the US, you can call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

A 7-day way to read your report

Checking once is trivia. Checking with a plan turns it into a decision. Here's a week that takes about two minutes a day:

DayDo this
Day 1Write down your guess for your daily average. Don't look yet.
Days 1–6Change nothing. Just use your phone like normal.
Day 7Open Daily Average → Week. Compare it with your guess.
Day 7Write down three things: your top app, your busiest time of day, and your top notification app.
Day 7Pick one change for one of the three, not all of them.

Then match the change to what you found. A top app that eats hours gets a limit; we walk through it in How to Set App Limits on iPhone That Actually Stick. A bad time of day gets a schedule. A loud app gets its notifications cut or summarized. If it's the late-night feed, the full playbook is in How to Stop Doomscrolling on iPhone.

Next Sunday, swipe back one week and compare. That's your real test, not a target someone else picked.
