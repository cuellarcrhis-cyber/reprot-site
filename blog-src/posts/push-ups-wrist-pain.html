Rep six. Your chest is fine. Your arms are fine. But there's a pinch on the back of your wrist every time you lower, and by rep ten it's the only thing you can think about.

So you stop, shake your hands out and wonder whether push-ups just aren't for you.

They probably are. Push-ups and wrist pain usually come from one thing you can change: a floor push-up asks your wrist to hold a lot of your body weight while it's bent back about as far as it goes. Change the angle, the load or the grip, and most people can keep training.

Below: why it happens, seven fixes in order from easiest to biggest change, how to build your wrists back up, and the signs that mean this isn't a form problem and you should get it checked.

Why push-ups cause wrist pain

Put your hand flat on a table and lean on it. Your wrist folds back hard. That's the position of a standard push-up, and then you put a large share of your body weight through it.

How large? In a 2011 force-plate study of 23 recreationally fit adults, a floor push-up put about 64% of body mass through the hands. A second small study found it peaks at around 75% at the bottom of the rep, which is also where the wrist is most bent.

≈64%Share of body mass on your hands in a floor push-up (Ebben et al., 2011, 23 adults). More at the bottom of the rep.

The best study on the wrist itself is small but useful. In 2018, a team of hand researchers had 14 volunteers do push-ups two ways, with the wrist bent back and with the wrist straight, and measured them with force plates and motion capture. The forces travelled differently through the joint: with the wrist bent back, they spread toward the back and the little-finger side of the wrist over a wider area.

The authors concluded that straight-wrist push-ups are "likely safer", because the bent-back position may load the wrist's ligaments more. It's a lab study of 14 people with no injury follow-up, so it can't tell you who will get hurt. But it supports what almost every fix below does: keep the wrist straighter, or put less weight on it.

First, rule out a stop sign

Most push-up wrist pain is a mild ache that goes away when you change position. Some isn't. Based on NHS guidance, stop push-ups and see a doctor if:

  • You have tingling, numbness or loss of feeling in your hand or fingers.
  • The pain stops you doing normal things, like opening a jar or typing.
  • It hasn't improved after two weeks of resting it at home.

Get help the same day (in the UK, NHS 111) if the pain is severe, you can't move your wrist or hold things, or you heard a snap, pop or grinding when it started. And if you've had a wrist, elbow or shoulder injury, arthritis in your hands, are pregnant, or haven't exercised in a long time, check with a doctor or physical therapist before you push through any of this.

A useful rule for everything else: a little awareness in the wrist is fine. Sharp pain, pain that builds rep by rep, or pain that's worse the next morning means change something before your next set.

1. Set your hands properly

Hands directly under your shoulders, or just outside them, not up by your face. Spread your fingers wide with the middle finger pointing forward or turned slightly out. Then press down through your whole hand, including the pads of your fingers and the base of your index finger, not just the heel of your palm.

Also check the rest of you. If your hips sag or your body shifts forward over your hands, the wrists take more of the work. Keep a straight line from head to heels and lower your chest between your hands.

2. Warm up the wrists

Two minutes before your first set:

  • Wrist circles: 10 each direction, hands loose.
  • Prayer stretch: palms together in front of your chest, slowly lower them until you feel a gentle stretch, hold 15–20 seconds.
  • Hands-and-knees rocks: on all fours, hands flat under your shoulders, gently rock your weight forward and back 10 times. Only go as far as feels comfortable.

None of this should hurt. If it does, skip straight to fix 5 or 7.

3. Raise your hands

An incline push-up isn't a lesser push-up. It's the same movement with less weight. In the same 2011 study, raising the hands onto a box about 61 cm (2 feet) high dropped the load on the hands from about 64% of body mass to about 41%. Knee push-ups came in around 49%.

Use a kitchen counter, a desk or a sturdy table, and lower the surface as your wrists settle. The full ladder of household surfaces is in How to Do Your First Real Push-Up.

4. Put a towel under the heel of your hand

Fold a small towel or the edge of your mat two or three times and rest the heel of each hand on it, fingers on the floor. It raises the palm slightly so the wrist bends back a little less. It's a small change, but for mild aches it's often enough. Keep it on a non-slip surface.

5. Do fist push-ups

Make a fist and rest on the flat part of your first two knuckles (index and middle finger), thumb wrapped outside. Your wrist stays straight, like the "neutral" position in the 2018 study. Do them on a mat or carpet, not a hard floor.

Keep the line from knuckles to forearm straight. If your wrist wobbles or tips to the side, go back to an incline: fists on a counter edge, or hold onto the edge itself.

6. Use handles or dumbbells

Push-up handles do the same job as fists with a softer grip. Hexagonal dumbbells work too, because they don't roll. Avoid round ones.

One honest note on fancy handles. A small 2009 study sponsored by the American Council on Exercise (13 trained male students) tested rotating push-up handles. They did increase chest, front-shoulder and triceps activity in standard and wide hand positions, likely because the handles let you go 4–5 inches deeper. But an exercise physiologist testing them found the pivoting made a straight wrist harder to hold. If wrists are your problem, fixed handles are the simpler choice.

Going deeper also means more work at the bottom of the rep, which is the heaviest part. Start with your usual depth.

7. Swap to a forearm plank for a week or two

If every version hurts, give the wrists a break without stopping altogether. A forearm plank works the same front-of-body muscles that hold your push-up line, with no load on the wrist. Hold for 15–30 seconds, a few times, stopping when your hips start to drop. Plank Variations for Beginners has easier versions, starting on the knees.

Then return to fix 3: high incline, short sets, and see how it feels the next day.

Build your wrists back up

Once the pain has settled, you can gently get your wrists used to the push-up position again. The idea is the same as any training: a little more each week, never into sharp pain.

  1. Week 1: Keep doing push-ups on fists, handles or an incline. Add the hands-and-knees rocks from fix 2 at the end of each session, 2 sets of 10.
  2. Week 2: Add a short flat-hand plank on a counter or table, 2 holds of 15–20 seconds.
  3. Week 3: Do your first set as flat-hand incline push-ups, the rest on fists or handles.
  4. Week 4 and on: Lower the surface or add flat-hand sets one at a time, as long as the next morning feels normal.

If any step brings the pain back, go back a week. And if your wrists are happiest on fists or handles, stay there. A push-up on a straight wrist still trains your chest, shoulders and arms. Nobody gives out points for flat palms.

The same goes for how much you do. Big jumps in daily volume are a common way to get sore joints; How Many Push-Ups Should You Do a Day? has a sensible daily number.

Questions people ask

Are knuckle push-ups better for your wrists?

For many people with wrist pain, yes, because the wrist stays straight. The 2018 study suggests a straight wrist spreads the force more evenly through the joint. They aren't better for everyone: some people find them hard on the knuckles, and they need a padded surface. Handles give you the same straight wrist with a softer grip.

Is it normal for wrists to hurt doing push-ups?

A mild ache when you're new to push-ups is common, because it's a position most of us rarely load. It isn't something to push through. If it doesn't ease with the fixes above within a couple of weeks, or you have numbness or tingling, get it checked.

Should I wear wrist wraps or a brace?

Some people like the support, but a wrap doesn't change the angle or the load, which are the two things causing the problem. Try fixes 3 to 6 first. If you've been given a brace for a diagnosed wrist problem, ask whoever gave it to you whether push-ups are OK.

TL;DR

  • Push-up wrist pain usually comes from holding a lot of body weight (about 64% on the floor) on a wrist bent back as far as it goes.
  • Start simple: hands under shoulders, fingers spread, press through the whole hand, and warm up the wrists.
  • Then reduce the load (incline push-ups) or straighten the wrist (towel, fists, fixed handles or hex dumbbells).
  • If everything hurts, swap to a forearm plank for a week or two and build back gradually.
  • Tingling, numbness, severe pain or pain that lasts two weeks means stop and see a doctor.
