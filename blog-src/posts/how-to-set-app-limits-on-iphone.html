You set a 30-minute limit on Instagram on Sunday night. It felt good.

Monday at 10:15 p.m., the gray screen appears. You tap the button under it without reading. Tuesday you do it at 9:50. By Thursday the limit is something your thumb clears on the way in, like a cookie banner.

That's not a willpower problem. Out of the box, app limits on iPhone are built to be dismissed. Apple's own Screen Time guide says it plainly: by default, limits can be ignored once you reach them.

So setting the limit is the easy part. Making it stick takes three more settings and one honest decision about how much time you actually want. Here's the whole setup, for iOS 27 and for iOS 26 and earlier.

How to set app limits on iPhone (iOS 27)

First, the name changed. In iOS 27, Apple folded App Limits and Downtime into two new tools: Time Allowances (how long you can use apps each day) and Screen Time Schedules (when they're available). If you're hunting for "App Limits" and can't find it, that's why. Apple notes these features need iOS 27 or later on all your Apple devices.

If Screen Time isn't on yet, open Settings → Screen Time and turn on App & Website Activity. Then:

  1. Go to Settings → Screen Time → Time Allowances.
  2. Tap the category that eats your day, such as Social Media or Entertainment.
  3. Drag the slider to your daily amount. You can also set an overall daily total across apps.
  4. If you only want the allowance to count during certain hours, turn on Match Screen Time Schedule.

Then set a schedule for your worst stretch of the day. Go to Settings → Screen Time → Screen Time Schedule, turn on Schedule, and add a time window (say 10:30 p.m. to 7 a.m.) with the distracting categories left out.

Finally, check Always Allowed. Apple lets you pick apps and contacts that stay available no matter what schedule or allowance is running. Keep Phone, Messages, Maps and anything you'd need in an emergency there, so you never have a reason to turn the whole thing off.

On iOS 26 or earlier

The same idea lives under older names. Go to Settings → Screen Time → App Limits → Add Limit, pick a category or open it to choose single apps, tap Next, set the time, and tap Add. Customize Days lets you give weekends a different amount. Your nightly window is Downtime.

The first two rows matter most. A limit with no passcode is a reminder. A reminder at 11 p.m., when you're tired, loses almost every time. We went into why that fight is rigged in Willpower Isn't the Problem.

Lock the limit with a Screen Time passcode

This is the setting that turns a limit into a limit. Go to Settings → Screen Time → Passcode & Management → Lock Screen Time Settings and create a four-digit passcode. With it on, changing your Screen Time settings asks for the code. Apple also lets you set up Screen Time Passcode Recovery with your Apple Account, so forgetting the code doesn't lock you out of your own settings.

Two rules for the code:

  • Don't reuse your phone passcode or any number you type all day. Your thumb will enter it before your brain gets a vote.
  • Strongest option: hand the phone to a partner, roommate or friend and let them type a code you don't see. It feels dramatic. It works, because now raising the limit means a conversation.

On iOS 26 and earlier, there's one more switch. Open the limit under App Limits and turn on Block at End of Limit, so that when time runs out you need the passcode to keep going. On iOS 27, check your allowance's settings after you add the passcode, and test it once: let a limit run out and see what the block screen asks for.

Passcode firstWithout a Screen Time passcode, an iPhone limit is a reminder you can dismiss in one tap.

Be honest about the ceiling here. Screen Time is a set of settings you control on your own phone. Someone determined to scroll can still find a way around it: the browser version, another device, asking for the code. The goal isn't a prison. It's making the mindless version slower than the choice you'd make if you were rested.

How much time should you allow?

Most people pick a number that sounds virtuous, like 15 minutes, hit it by lunch and turn the whole thing off by Friday. Pick a number you can live with instead.

Check your real numbers first: open Settings → Screen Time and look at a full week of activity, not just today. Then use this as a starting point:

Your daily average on the appWeek 1 limitWeek 3 target
3+ hours90 minutes60 minutes
About 2 hours60 minutes40 minutes
About 1 hour30 minutes20 minutes
Under 30 minutesSkip the limitUse a schedule for your worst hour

These aren't clinical numbers; they're a way to cut roughly in half and then step down, so the limit survives your first bad night. If you blow through one, loosen it by 10 minutes and keep the passcode. A loose limit that holds beats a strict one you delete.

Do app limits actually reduce screen time?

Somewhat, and the research is more modest than the marketing.

In a 2026 randomized trial in JMIR mHealth and uHealth, 70 iPhone users who regularly used social media were split into two groups for three weeks. The intervention group used an app that showed a full-screen reminder when a session ran past a limit they'd set themselves. They cut about 29 minutes a day from their most problematic app compared with the control group. But scores for problematic social media use didn't significantly improve, and three weeks is short. The authors call it short-term evidence.

About 29 min/dayLess time on the most problematic app with self-set limit reminders, in a 3-week trial of 70 iPhone users (Mertens et al., 2026).

A 2023 study in PNAS looked at a different kind of friction: a short delay and a prompt before a chosen app opens. Across the study, people backed out of 36% of their attempts to open the target app, and by week six they were trying to open it 37% less often than in week one. The part that mattered most was the simple option to back out. The waiting time helped too; the reminder message alone didn't. These were people who chose to install the tool, so they were motivated to begin with.

Put together: a limit works best as a pause that asks you to choose, not a scold. The more that pause asks of you, the more often you take the exit.

Give the limit a price instead of a wall

When the limit hits, the urge to scroll doesn't vanish. It needs somewhere to go. So give it a job: before you ask for more time, do one short set. Ten squats, or a set of push-ups.

Keep it easy enough that you'll do it at 11 p.m.:

  • Squats: feet about shoulder-width, sit back like there's a chair behind you, chest up, knees tracking over your toes. Easier: sit down onto an actual chair and stand back up.
  • Push-ups: hands under shoulders, body straight, lower your chest toward the floor. Easier: hands on a kitchen counter or a wall, or knees on the floor.
  • Stop if anything hurts. If you have an injury, joint pain, a heart condition, are pregnant, or haven't been active in a long time, check with a doctor before adding exercise.

A minute of movement a few times a day is worth more than it sounds; we covered the research on exercise snacks.

TL;DR

  • In iOS 27, App Limits became Time Allowances: Settings → Screen Time → Time Allowances. On iOS 26, use Settings → Screen Time → App Limits → Add Limit.
  • By default, limits can be ignored once you reach them. A Screen Time passcode is what makes them hold.
  • Have someone else set the passcode, and test it once by letting a limit run out.
  • Limit the category, not just one app, and add a schedule for your worst hour.
  • Start at about half your real daily average, then step down. Loose limits that hold beat strict ones you delete.
  • Research suggests limits trim time modestly; a pause that asks you to choose works better than a scold.
