6:45. You're up. Technically. You're sitting on the edge of the bed with your phone in your hand, and the plan was to "do a quick workout before work."

The plan was a 30-minute video. It needs a mat, a cleared living room and a version of you who's awake. You have six minutes and a strip of carpet between the bed and the wall.

That strip is enough. So is the bed.

This is a 5-minute morning workout built for the space next to your bed and for a body that woke up four minutes ago. Five moves, one minute each, no equipment. The bed is the only gear, and every step has an easier version for the days you feel like furniture.

The 5-minute morning workout, minute by minute

Here's the whole routine. Read the table once, then do it. The form notes are in the next section.

MinuteMoveEasier versionHarder version
1Sit, stand, march. 20–30 seconds sitting on the edge of the bed, feet flat. Stand up slowly, then march in place with big arm circles.Stay seated and march your feet while you circle your arms.Brisk knee-high marching for the last 30 seconds.
2Squats to the bed. Sit back until you lightly touch the mattress, stand up. 10–15 slow reps.Sit all the way down, then stand. Use your hands on your thighs if you need to.Hover just above the bed without touching it.
3Incline push-ups. Hands on a sturdy bed frame or the wall. 5–12 reps.Wall push-ups, feet a small step away from the wall.Hands on the floor, knees or toes.
4Glute bridges. On the floor, knees bent, lift your hips and squeeze for a second. 10–15 reps.Smaller lift, or lie on the bed and do them there.Pause for 3 seconds at the top of each rep.
5Plank, then reach. 20–30 seconds of plank, then stand and reach overhead, up on your toes, for the rest of the minute.Plank with your hands on the bed frame or a wall.Forearm plank on the floor, up to 45 seconds.

Rest whenever you need to. The minute is a container, not a test. If you finish 10 squats in 35 seconds, stand and breathe for the other 25.

Why this order: you start seated, then work up from the easiest big move (squats) to the floor moves, so your blood pressure and your brain get a few minutes to catch up with being vertical.

How to do each move (half-asleep edition)

Minute 1: sit, stand, march

Don't skip the sitting part. After a night lying down, some people get lightheaded when they stand up fast. Health guides on this suggest rolling to your side, pushing up to sitting, waiting a bit, then standing slowly. Harvard Health also suggests clenching your leg muscles before you stand. So: sit, feet flat, squeeze your thighs a few times, then stand. If the room spins, sit back down.

Minute 2: squats to the bed

Feet about shoulder-width apart, toes turned out a little. Sit your hips back like you're aiming for the mattress, chest up, heels down. Touch the bed lightly, don't flop onto it, and stand back up. The bed is a depth guide, so you don't have to judge how low to go while you're still foggy. For the full checklist, see bodyweight squat form.

Minute 3: incline push-ups

A mattress is soft and a bed can slide, so put your hands on the solid part: the wooden or metal frame, a footboard, or a heavy dresser. If none of those are sturdy, use the wall. Body in one straight line from head to heels, lower your chest toward your hands, push back. The higher your hands, the easier it is. Move your hands lower as it gets easy.

Minute 4: glute bridges

Lie on your back on the floor, knees bent, feet flat and close to your hips. Push through your heels and lift your hips until your body makes a line from shoulders to knees. Squeeze for a second, lower slowly. Don't arch your lower back to get higher; the squeeze does the work, not the height.

Minute 5: plank, then reach

Forearms or hands under your shoulders, body straight, squeeze your glutes and brace like someone's about to poke your stomach. Hold 20–30 seconds. Then stand, reach your arms overhead and rise up onto your toes a few times. You finish standing, which is the point. Short, solid holds beat long, saggy ones.

Safety, plainly: move slowly, stop if anything hurts (pain is different from effort), and if you're pregnant, have a heart condition, an injury, or haven't exercised in a long time, check with a doctor before starting. If you ever faint or fall when you stand up, tell your doctor.

Is a 5-minute workout enough?

Enough to count. Not enough to be the whole week.

The 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans dropped the old rule that activity only counted in bouts of 10 minutes or more. The CDC's summary says adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate-to-vigorous activity get some health benefit.

The full target is still bigger: at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, plus muscle-strengthening work for all the major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week. Five minutes every morning is 35 minutes a week. That's a real start and covers the muscle groups, lightly. It isn't the finish line.

Two honest ways to grow it: add a second round on days you have ten minutes, and add short bursts later in the day, the way exercise snacks work. The morning five is the anchor, not the ceiling.

Will a quick morning workout actually wake you up?

Probably a little, for a little while. The best direct test is small but interesting.

In a 2017 University of Georgia study in Physiology & Behavior, 18 young women who slept under 6.5 hours a night tried three things on different days: a 50 mg caffeine capsule (about a can of soda), a placebo, and 10 minutes of easy-paced stair walking. The stairs raised their feelings of energy more than the caffeine did.

The caveats matter. The boost was short-lived; an hour later the groups felt about the same. The stairs didn't improve attention or memory. And 18 sleep-short college women don't tell us much about everyone else. So: a nudge, not a fix for a short night.

It also won't erase the first-thing fog. Sleep scientists call it sleep inertia, and a Sleep Research Society fact sheet says it usually fades within about 20 minutes. Moving doesn't skip that window, but it does mean you spend it standing up instead of scrolling in bed. We dig into that in your alarm isn't too quiet.

Set it up the night before

The workout isn't the hard part. Starting it is. Decide everything while your judgment works, which is not at 6:45.

  1. Phone across the room. Charge it on a dresser, not the nightstand. If it's your alarm, you have to stand up to stop it, and you're already in position for minute 1.
  2. Clear the strip. A body-length patch of floor next to the bed, nothing to trip on. Laundry on that patch is a decision you'll lose.
  3. Clothes on top. Whatever you'll move in, folded where you'll see it. Pajamas work too, honestly.
  4. Pick the anchor. "After I turn off the alarm, I sit on the edge of the bed." The workout starts from something you already do every day.

A 4-week ladder if five minutes feels like a lot

If the full routine is too much on day one, build it.

  • Week 1: minutes 1 and 2 only. Sit, stand, squats to the bed. Two minutes.
  • Week 2: add minute 3, wall or bed-frame push-ups.
  • Week 3: add the bridges.
  • Week 4: the full five minutes. Then start moving one move per week to its harder version.

Missed a morning? Do minute 2 at lunch and call it a day. Shrinking the workout beats skipping it, which is the whole argument of adjust, don't miss.

TL;DR

  • Five one-minute blocks next to your bed: sit-stand-march, squats to the bed, incline push-ups, glute bridges, plank then reach.
  • Start seated for 20–30 seconds and stand slowly; some people get lightheaded standing up after sleep.
  • Use a sturdy bed frame or the wall for push-ups, not the mattress.
  • Five minutes counts, but it's 35 of the 150 weekly minutes the guidelines suggest. Treat it as the anchor.
  • A small trial found 10 minutes of easy stair walking felt more energizing than 50 mg of caffeine, briefly. Expect a nudge, not a miracle.
  • Set it up the night before: phone across the room, floor clear.
