You've got 15 minutes, a living room and no weights. You search for a leg workout and get a 40-minute video with jump lunges, pistol squats and a resistance band you don't own.

So you do a few squats, wonder if lunges would have been better, try a wall sit, quit at 25 seconds, and go back to your phone.

The problem isn't your legs. It's that nobody told you which move to pick.

Here's the short version of a no-equipment leg workout: squats are the base, lunges and split squats are where you go once squats get easy, and wall sits are the hold you add when your knees don't like moving or you want a hard finish. Below is what each one is good for, what the research actually says, and a 15-minute session at three levels you can do tonight.

The best no-equipment leg workout: which move to pick

If you only do one, do squats. They train the most muscle at once, they're the easiest to learn, and there's a version for every level, from standing up out of a chair to a slow, paused squat.

The three moves aren't rivals. They do different jobs.

Squats: the base of any bodyweight leg workout

The squat works your thighs and glutes together and copies something you do all day: sit down, stand up. That's why every top workout starts with it, and why it's the easiest to scale.

The short form check: feet about shoulder-width, hips back and down, knees following your toes, heels down, stand all the way up. The full six steps and the common mistakes are in Bodyweight Squat Form.

The catch is that squats get easy. Most people who stick with them hit a point where 20 reps barely register. You don't need more reps at that point. You need a harder version: slow the lowering to three seconds, pause at the bottom, or move to one leg.

Squats or lunges: which is better?

Neither, on strength. The difference is how fast each one runs out of challenge.

Two small trials compared one-leg and two-leg training directly:

  • In a 2005 study in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 38 untrained men and women did either one-leg or two-leg squat training, matched for volume and intensity. The authors concluded both were equally effective for early gains in leg strength and power.
  • In a 2016 study, 18 academy rugby players trained twice a week for five weeks with either back squats or rear-foot-elevated split squats. The authors found the two similarly effective for lower-body strength, 40-meter sprint speed and change of direction.

Both studies used weights, both were small, and nobody has run the same comparison with bodyweight only. Read them as "one-leg work isn't second best," not as proof of anything bigger.

What matters at home is load. In a split squat, one leg carries most of your body weight, so it gets much harder without adding anything. That's the reason to move to lunges: not because they're better, but because they keep working after squats stop.

Lunge or split squat first?

Start with the split squat. Your feet stay planted in a long stance and you just lower straight down and stand back up, so there's less to balance. Hold a counter or the back of a chair if you wobble. Once that's steady, reverse lunges (step back, lower, step forward) add the balance part. Forward and walking lunges come last.

Cues for all of them: front heel stays down, front knee tracks over the middle of your foot, torso tall or leaning slightly forward, lower until the back knee is just above the floor, or as far as is comfortable.

Wall sits vs squats: what a hold is good for

A wall sit is a squat that doesn't move. Your back is flat on the wall, feet out in front, and you slide down until your knees are bent, then hold.

For building strength through a full range, squats win. Holds build strength mostly around the position you hold, and a 2019 systematic review of 26 studies found that holds at longer muscle lengths (deeper positions) built more muscle and carried over better to moving strength. A shallow wall sit mostly trains a shallow wall sit.

So why do it? Three reasons:

  • It's gentle on knees that complain. No moving under load, and you choose the depth. Many people can hold a high wall sit when deep squats bother them.
  • It's a finisher. After squats and lunges, a hold lets you work tired legs without form falling apart rep by rep.
  • It's a bridge to deeper squats. Holding a bit lower each week gets you used to the bottom position.

The blood pressure study

Wall sits got a lot of attention after a 2023 review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine. Researchers pooled 270 randomized trials with 15,827 participants and compared exercise types on resting blood pressure. Every type lowered it on average. Isometric training (holds like wall sits and planks) showed the biggest average drop, about 8.2/4.0 mmHg, compared with about 4.6/3.0 mmHg for moving resistance training like squats. In a secondary analysis, the wall sit ranked first of the individual exercises for lowering systolic pressure.

270 trialsIn a 2023 meta-analysis, holds like wall sits showed the largest average drop in resting blood pressure of any exercise type (Edwards et al.).

Two honest caveats. The authors note the trials differed a lot in who took part and how they trained, and the per-exercise rankings rest on fewer trials than the main result. And this is general information, not a treatment: if you have high blood pressure, talk to your doctor before starting, and don't change any medication on your own. Blood pressure also rises during a hard hold and settles soon after, which is why the breathing rule below matters.

A 15-minute no-equipment leg workout, by level

Warm up first: 2 minutes of marching on the spot, 10 slow half squats and 10 standing leg swings each side.

Then do the three moves in order. Rest about 60 seconds between sets. End every set with two or three good reps still in the tank, and stop when form breaks: heels lift, knees cave in, or the reps get shallow.

Finish with a set of 15 calf raises (hold a wall) if you want the lower legs covered too.

This table is a conservative rule of thumb, not a result from a study. Move up a row when the top of your range feels easy for two sessions in a row.

How often

Two or three times a week is plenty. The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines ask adults for muscle-strengthening work on two or more days a week, covering all the major muscle groups. Pair this with push, pull and core work, like the plan in Bodyweight Strength Training at Home. If you want something on the days in between, a single easy set of squats is fine; the details are in Squats Every Day.

Safety notes

  • Breathe during wall sits. Don't hold your breath and strain.
  • Joint pain means stop. Muscle burn is normal. Sharp pain in the knee, hip or back is not. Go higher on the wall, shorten the range, or swap the move.
  • Hold something for one-leg moves. A counter or a sturdy chair against a wall.
  • Skip jumping versions until the plain ones are easy.

Check with a doctor before starting if you have a heart condition or high blood pressure, a knee, hip or back injury, are pregnant, or haven't exercised in a long time.

Questions people ask

Can you build leg muscle with no equipment?

Yes, for most people starting out, as long as the sets keep getting harder, and one-leg moves get you there without weights. In a small 2023 trial of 13 sedentary women, covered in our bodyweight strength guide, progressive bodyweight squats built leg strength and muscle about as well as barbell squats over six weeks. Very strong legs eventually need added load.

How long should I hold a wall sit?

Start with 15–30 seconds and add 5 seconds when it feels manageable. Two holds of 45–60 seconds at a comfortable depth is a solid target for most people. Going deeper matters more than going longer once you can hold a minute.

Are lunges bad for your knees?

Not for healthy knees with good form. Knee trouble in lunges usually comes from a front knee caving inward, a heel lifting, or too much too soon. Start with supported split squats and a short range. If your knee still hurts, wall sits at a comfortable depth are a gentler option while you get it checked.

TL;DR

  • For a no-equipment leg workout, start with squats, add a one-leg move once squats get easy, and finish with a wall sit.
  • One-leg and two-leg training gave similar strength gains in two small trials; split squats just stay hard longer at home.
  • Wall sits are gentle on complaining knees and showed the largest average drop in resting blood pressure in a 270-trial review. Breathe through them and check with a doctor if your blood pressure is high.
  • 15 minutes, 2–3 times a week: 3 sets of squats, 2 sets of a one-leg move each side, 2 wall sit holds, scaled to your level.
  • Stop for joint pain, hold something for balance, and skip the jumping versions until the basics are easy.
