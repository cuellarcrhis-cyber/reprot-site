You sit down to work. Laptop open, coffee, a real plan for the next hour.

Your phone buzzes. You check it "for a second." Eleven minutes later you're watching a stranger pressure-wash a driveway, and the plan is gone.

Brian Chesky, co-founder and CEO of Airbnb, has a blunt explanation for this. In a clip from the Silicon Valley Girl Podcast, he puts it in one line:

"The phone is an enemy to focus, and it's not designed [to be] something you're staring at for hours a day."

Then he offers a rule that's simple enough to use today.

Attention span shrinks with the screen

Chesky's argument is a ladder. "The bigger the screen, the longer the attention span":

  • IMAX theater: you're not even going to get up for the bathroom.
  • Living-room TV: you watch a show, but you might get up.
  • Computer: you'll watch a full YouTube film.
  • Phone: "you'll only be able to pay attention for 15 seconds before you scroll to the next video."

The host's reaction: "Attention span is commensurate with screen size. I never thought about that." His rule follows from it:

"If you want to focus, you need to have a long attention span. If you want to have a long attention span, have a big screen. Do not look at a phone if you want to focus."
Big screen, long focusBrian Chesky's rule: do focus work on the biggest screen you have, and keep the phone out of it.

Is it really about screen size?

Not exactly, and that's the useful part. A phone can hold your attention for hours if you're reading a book on it. The difference is what each screen is built and used for.

A movie theater gives you one thing, in the dark, with no next option. A phone gives you a feed where the next thing is always one swipe away, plus notifications that pull you out of whatever you were doing. Small screens also travel with you into every gap in your day, so they become the default for every bored minute. That's what trains the 15-second habit, not the size of the glass.

So read Chesky's rule as: do focused things on devices and in places built for one thing at a time.

What the research says

Chesky's ladder is an observation, not a study. But it lines up with what researchers have measured.

Our attention on screens has gotten shorter. Gloria Mark, a professor at UC Irvine, has tracked how long people stay on one screen before switching for about two decades. In her early studies the average was around two and a half minutes. In studies from 2016 to 2020 it was about 47 seconds. Those aren't the same people followed over time, and it isn't a clinical "attention span" test, but the direction is clear. She has also found that about half of these switches are self-interruptions: nobody pinged us, we just went.

47 secondsAverage time on one screen before switching, in Gloria Mark's studies from 2016 to 2020 (down from about 2.5 minutes in the early 2000s).

Short videos are linked to weaker focus. A 2025 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin pooled 71 studies and nearly 100,000 people. Heavier short-form video use was associated with poorer attention and inhibitory control (the ability to stop yourself doing something). Two big caveats: it's correlational, so it can't prove short videos cause the problem, and many studies relied on people rating their own attention.

How to use the big-screen rule

You don't need an IMAX. You need to stop doing deep work within reach of a feed. Five practical moves:

  1. Put focus work on the biggest screen you have. Writing, studying, coding, planning: laptop or desktop, full screen, one window.
  2. Move the phone out of reach, not just face-down. Another room is best. Out of reach turns "check it for a second" into a decision.
  3. Turn on a Focus. In Settings → Focus, set up a Work or Do Not Disturb focus that silences everything except the people you choose. A notification you don't see can't break your concentration.
  4. Watch long things on big screens. If you want to watch something, watch a whole thing on a TV or computer instead of 40 clips on your phone.
  5. Make the feed cost something. Limit the apps that turn into scroll holes. We walk through the exact settings in How to Stop Doomscrolling on iPhone.

The phone isn't the villain everywhere

Your phone is still great for calls, maps, paying for coffee and messaging the people you love. The point isn't to throw it in a lake. It's to stop letting a device built for quick hits sit in the middle of the work that needs your longest attention.

And when the urge to check it hits, notice what it actually is. Usually it isn't curiosity. It's a small itch to escape something hard. A short, physical break deals with that itch better than a feed does; we explain why in Willpower Isn't the Problem.

TL;DR

  • Brian Chesky: "The phone is an enemy to focus." The bigger the screen, the longer the attention span.
  • His rule: if you want to focus, use a big screen and don't look at your phone.
  • It's less about screen size than about feeds and notifications always one swipe away.
  • Research shows shrinking time on any one screen (about 47 seconds in Gloria Mark's work) and links heavy short-video use to weaker attention.
  • Do focus work on a computer, keep the phone in another room, turn on a Focus, and limit the feeds.
