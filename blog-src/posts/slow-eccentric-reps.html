It's 10:40 p.m. You skipped the workout again because you didn't have 45 minutes, and a workout that isn't 45 minutes doesn't feel like it counts.

So you do nothing. Again.

Here's a different plan. Stand in front of a chair. Take five full seconds to sit down. Stand up normally. Do that ten times. Now do the same slow lowering with a push-up against the wall. That's most of a short daily workout that researchers have actually tested, and it takes about five minutes.

The trick isn't the exercises. It's the speed on the way down. Slowing the lowering part of a rep (the "eccentric" phase) makes a few easy-looking reps do more work. And a growing body of research says you don't need to be exhausted, or sore, for training to pay off.

Here's what the studies found, where they're thin, and a routine you can start tonight.

What "slow eccentric reps" means

Every rep has two halves. The lifting half, when the muscle shortens: standing up out of a squat, pushing away from the floor. And the lowering half, when the muscle lengthens under control: sitting down, bending your arms toward the floor.

The lowering half is the eccentric phase. Most people rush it, or let gravity do it. Slow eccentric reps flip that: you take 3–5 seconds to lower, then come back up at a normal speed.

Two things make the lowering phase interesting. Your muscles can produce more force while lengthening than while shortening, which is why you can lower a weight you couldn't lift. And they do it at a lower energy cost, so it feels easier than it is. That combination is what Ken Nosaka, a professor of exercise science at Edith Cowan University (ECU) in Australia, has spent decades studying.

Does a 5-minute workout actually do anything?

The study behind the recent headlines is a 2025 ECU trial led by Brad Kirk, with Nosaka as a co-author. Twenty-two sedentary adults aged 32 to 69 (18 women, 4 men) did the same four bodyweight moves every day for four weeks: chair squats, a chair recline (a slow lean back from a seat), wall push-ups and heel drops. Ten reps each, with a five-second lowering. About five minutes a day, at home.

After four weeks, on average:

  • Leg and back strength (an isometric pull test) was up about 13%.
  • Push-ups to failure were up about 66%, and sit-ups about 51%.
  • Sit-and-reach flexibility was up about 9%.
  • Scores on two mental health and vitality questionnaires rose by about 16% and 20%.
  • People stuck with it: they did about 91% of the sessions.

Now the honest part. This was a small trial with no separate control group. Each person was measured over a two-week period of normal life first, then compared with themselves after the program. The averages hide a huge spread: some people improved a lot, some barely at all. Body composition, resting heart rate and blood pressure didn't change significantly. And it didn't measure muscle size.

So the fair reading is this: five minutes of slow reps a day made untrained adults measurably stronger and fitter at those tasks in a month, and they actually did it. It's not proof that five minutes beats a real program. It's proof that five minutes beats nothing, by more than you'd think.

The bigger idea: it doesn't have to hurt

In 2026, Nosaka published a review in the Journal of Sport and Health Science arguing that eccentric exercise should be the "new normal", not a niche tool for athletes. ECU's summary of it: strenuous exercise and muscle soreness aren't needed to improve muscle size, strength or performance. In Nosaka's words, "The idea that exercise must be exhausting or painful is holding people back."

That lines up with older evidence. A 2009 meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found eccentric training done at higher intensities built more strength than lifting-focused training, with a trend toward more muscle growth. The authors put the edge down mostly to the higher loads muscles can handle while lowering.

Why daily beats heroic

The routine that gets done wins. That's the point a lot of people took from Skye Mackintosh (@dailyrepsguy), whose 20-minute workouts filmed every single day were tied to the ECU research in LADbible's coverage this year. By then the streak had passed 260 days. We wrote about Skye's "adjust, don't miss" rule in How to Stay Consistent With Working Out.

There's lab evidence for small and often, too. In a 2022 ECU study, people who did a handful of maximal eccentric arm contractions five days a week gained more strength than people who did a larger number once a week. Six maximal contractions once a week produced no significant change at all. It was an arm-machine study, not a home workout, but the pattern is the same: frequency did more than a big weekly dose.

Translation: a five-minute set you'll actually do every day is worth more than the hour-long session you keep moving to Monday.

A short daily workout: the 5-minute slow-lowering routine

Do this once a day. Ten reps of each move, one after another. Count the lowering out loud if it helps: "one-thousand, two-thousand…" Start with a three-second lowering; build to five over a couple of weeks. Come back up at a normal pace, using your hands if you need to.

MoveThe slow partEasierHarder
Chair squatSit down to the chair over 3–5 s, then standHigher seat, or stand up using your handsLower seat, or a slow squat with no chair
Wall push-upBend your arms and bring your chest toward the wall over 3–5 s, then push backStand closer to the wallHands on a counter, then knees, then the floor
Chair reclineSit tall near the front of the seat, arms crossed, and lean back toward the backrest over 3–5 s; use your hands to sit back upSmaller lean, hands resting on your thighsLean back further before you stop
Heel dropRise onto your toes, then lower your heels over 3–5 s, one hand on a wallSmaller riseDo it on the edge of a step, heels dropping below it

Form cues that matter:

  • Chair squat: feet about shoulder-width, knees pointing the same way as your toes, chest up. Control the whole way down; don't drop onto the seat for the last few inches. Our squat form guide covers the details.
  • Wall push-up: body in a straight line from head to heels, elbows angled back rather than flared straight out.
  • Chair recline: keep your back long and stop the lean where you can still control it. No jerking to sit back up.
  • Heel drop: slow and smooth, no bouncing at the bottom.

How to progress

When ten slow reps of a move feel easy, move one column to the right. Don't chase longer and longer lowering times. A 2015 meta-analysis of eight studies found that reps lasting anywhere from half a second to about eight seconds built muscle similarly when sets were hard, while very slow reps (over ten seconds) looked worse, though the evidence there is thin. Three to five seconds down is a good tool for making easy moves harder. It isn't magic past that.

For push-ups, the slow-lowering version is also the best bridge from the wall to the floor. The full progression is in How to Do Your First Real Push-Up.

What this routine won't do

Five minutes a day is a floor, not a ceiling. It won't match a full strength program for building muscle, and the current evidence is in untrained people over a few weeks. If you want more, the US Physical Activity Guidelines recommend strength work for all major muscle groups on at least two days a week, and a harder session like the 100-rep method on two of your days fits well alongside it.

Questions people ask

How slow should the lowering be?

Three to five seconds. The ECU study used five. Start at three if five feels shaky, and keep it controlled the whole way rather than slow at the top and dropping at the end.

Will slow reps make me sore?

Maybe a little in the first week, especially your thighs and calves. Eccentric work is the type most likely to cause soreness when it's new, and the effect fades quickly with repeat sessions: muscles adapt so the same work causes much less soreness the next time. Ten reps per move is a small dose on purpose. Mild aches are normal; soreness that lasts more than a few days means you did too much, so ease off.

Do I have to do it every day?

The study did, and that's part of why it worked: a daily habit is easier to keep than a schedule. But missing a day doesn't undo anything. On a bad day, do one move. On a good day, do two rounds.

Safety first: if you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, a joint injury, balance problems, are pregnant, or haven't exercised in a long time, check with a doctor before starting. Use a sturdy chair that won't slide, and keep a hand near a wall for heel drops. Stop for sharp pain, joint pain, dizziness or chest pain.

TL;DR

  • Slow eccentric reps: take 3–5 seconds to lower, come up at normal speed. It makes a few easy moves do more work.
  • In a small 2025 trial, 22 sedentary adults who did about five minutes a day for four weeks got stronger, fitter at push-ups and sit-ups, and reported better mental health. No control group, and results varied widely.
  • The routine: 10 slow reps each of chair squats, wall push-ups, chair reclines and heel drops, daily.
  • Progress by making the move harder, not the lowering longer: over about 10 seconds a rep looks worse, not better.
  • It doesn't have to hurt. Mild soreness early on is normal; sharp or joint pain means stop.
