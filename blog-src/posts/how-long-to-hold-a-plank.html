You prop your phone against the couch and start a one-minute timer. The first 20 seconds are fine.

At 0:35 your arms start to shake. At 0:45 your hips sink toward the carpet and your lower back takes over. You hold your breath and grind out the last 15 seconds anyway, because the timer said a minute.

Those last 15 seconds probably did more for your ego than your core.

So how long should you hold a plank? Shorter than the internet tells you, and more than once. Here's the number for your level, what the research actually says about short versus long holds, what a "normal" plank time looks like, and easier versions if a full plank isn't there yet.

How long to hold a plank: the short answer

Hold a plank only as long as you can keep a straight line and breathe normally. When your hips sag or your back starts to ache, the set is over, whatever the clock says.

For most people that means two or three holds with a short rest between them, not one marathon. Mayo Clinic's guidance for a plank is to hold for 30 to 60 seconds and repeat two to three times. If you're new, start lower than that.

Why short holds with rest beat one long one

The best-known voice on this is Stuart McGill, a spine biomechanics researcher at the University of Waterloo. In a 2018 interview with The Telegraph he argued that very long planks have no real use beyond setting a record, and that repeated holds of about 10 seconds are best for most people.

He pointed to his own lab's research. In a 2015 study, Lee and McGill put 24 healthy young men through six weeks of core training: one group did isometric exercises (holds, like a plank), one did moving exercises, and one did neither. The isometric group showed the biggest increase in torso stiffness, the kind of steadiness that helps your trunk hold its shape under load. The moving group improved less; the control group didn't change.

Be honest about what that study is. It was small, all young men, and it measured stiffness in a lab, not plank time, back pain or anything you'd notice day to day. It didn't compare 10-second planks with one-minute planks head to head. Plenty of trainers still recommend 30 to 60 seconds, and Mayo Clinic's guidance sits there too.

The useful part doesn't depend on who's right about the exact number: several shorter, clean holds give you more good-form seconds than one long hold that falls apart at the end. That's the same idea behind grease the groove: practice quality, stop well before failure, repeat.

What's a normal plank time?

The largest set of plank numbers comes from a 2014 study of 471 college students in the U.S., who held a forearm plank until they quit or their form failed. Men averaged about two minutes (124 seconds) and women about 83 seconds. Varsity athletes averaged 123 seconds, everyone else 83.

83 secondsAverage plank among non-athlete college students held to failure (Strand et al., 2014). The spread was huge.

Don't turn that into a target. The spread was wide (the typical gap from the average was about a minute either way), everyone was college-aged, and they held until they couldn't. The authors themselves say more research is needed before using their numbers for other groups. A 40-year-old who hasn't exercised in a while shouldn't measure themselves against a 20-year-old on a sports team.

The number worth tracking is your own: how long you can hold good form today, compared with two weeks ago.

How to do a plank with good form

  1. Forearms down. Elbows directly under your shoulders, forearms flat or hands loosely together.
  2. Legs back. Step your feet back, toes tucked, feet about hip-width apart. Wider feet make it a little easier.
  3. Make one line. Head, shoulders, hips and heels in a straight line. Eyes on the floor just in front of your hands.
  4. Brace. Tighten your stomach like you're about to be poked, and squeeze your glutes. That keeps your lower back from sagging.
  5. Breathe. Slow breaths in and out. If you can only hold it by holding your breath, the hold is too long or too hard.

When to end the set

  • Your hips sink toward the floor, or pike up toward the ceiling.
  • You feel it in your lower back more than your stomach.
  • Your shoulders creep up toward your ears or your head drops.
  • You're holding your breath.

Any one of those means you're done. Drop to your knees, rest, and start the next hold fresh. Filming a few seconds from the side with your phone is the fastest way to see a sag you can't feel.

Easier and harder versions

If a floor plank shakes apart in under 10 seconds, start lower on this ladder. Move up a step when you can do three steady holds of 20 to 30 seconds.

  1. Wall plank. Forearms on a wall at shoulder height, step your feet back until your body leans in one line.
  2. Counter plank. Forearms on a kitchen counter or sturdy table. The lower the surface, the harder it gets.
  3. Knee plank. Forearms and knees on the floor, one line from head to knees. Mayo Clinic's beginner plank starts here.
  4. Forearm plank. The full version above.
  5. High plank. Straight arms, hands under shoulders. It's the top of a push-up held still, which is why planks help with your first real push-up.

Past 60 clean seconds, make it harder instead of longer: lift one foot an inch for a few seconds, then switch; tap one shoulder at a time from a high plank; or turn sideways into a side plank, where Mayo Clinic suggests holds of 10 to 30 seconds.

A 4-week plank plan

Three or four days a week is plenty. The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening work on two or more days a week, and short plank holds count toward that for your core. Use whatever step of the ladder you're on.

Safety notes

  • Effort, not pain. Shaking and a burning stomach are normal. Sharp pain in your lower back, shoulders or wrists means stop.
  • Wrists: forearm planks are easier on them than high planks. A folded towel under your forearms helps on hard floors.
  • Keep breathing. Slow, steady breaths through every hold. If you have to hold your breath to finish, end the set.
  • Check with a doctor first if you have a back or shoulder injury, a heart condition or high blood pressure, are pregnant or recently gave birth, or haven't exercised in a long time. Start with the wall plank.

Questions people ask

Is a 1-minute plank good?

Yes, if it's a clean minute. It's a solid goal for most people and the top of Mayo Clinic's suggested range. Past that, a harder version gives you more for your time than a longer one.

Should you plank every day?

Short, easy holds most days are fine for many people. If your back or shoulders feel worn out, take a day off. Two to four days a week is enough to make progress.

Why do I shake during a plank?

Shaking is common as muscles tire, especially when the exercise is new. If your line holds and nothing hurts, it's not a problem. If your hips start to drop, end the set.

TL;DR

  • Hold a plank only as long as you keep a straight line and breathe normally. When form breaks, the set is over.
  • Beginners: 3 holds of 10–20 seconds, on your knees or an incline if needed. Most people: 2–3 holds of 30–60 seconds.
  • Several short, clean holds beat one long, sagging one. Past 60 clean seconds, make it harder, not longer.
  • The "average" from a big college study (83 seconds for non-athletes) isn't a target; track your own clean time instead.
  • Check with a doctor first if you have a back injury, a heart condition or high blood pressure, or are pregnant.
