## Search intent

"Why do I grab my phone the second I'm bored, and is that actually a problem?" They want the reason in plain words and something to do differently, in the first section.

## Must-cover

- What boredom is (a signal, not a malfunction)
- Why the phone is the default response (always there, instant, habit loop)
- Whether the phone actually relieves boredom (it mostly doesn't)
- Benefits of some boredom (mind-wandering, creativity), honestly sized
- Practical ways to sit with it / redirect it
- When boredom is more than boredom (apathy, depression) → professional help

## Our edge

1. The 2026 Essex study (Tam, Van Tilburg & Chan): it's *disliking* boredom, not boredom itself, that predicts more phone use. That makes the lever an attitude you can train, which no competitor builds the post around.
2. The published 2021 Dora et al. paper with real numbers and caveats (83 PhD students, 1.43× more likely to pick up, boredom slightly higher afterward), not the 2020 preprint summary.
3. The Wilson 2014 shock study told honestly, including the Fox et al. critique.
4. A "what is the boredom telling you?" table: signal → better answer, plus a 2-minute rule and a physical outlet with scale-downs.

## Facts used

- Eastwood, Frischen, Fenske & Smilek 2012, Perspectives on Psychological Science 7(5):482–495: boredom = wanting, but being unable, to engage in satisfying activity (abstract + APS release).
- Tam, Van Tilburg & Chan 2026, Motivation and Emotion 50(5):972–984, doi 10.1007/s11031-026-10230-9: Study 1 correlational n=495; Study 2 three-wave longitudinal n=261, four months apart; boredom dislike predicted more frequent and intense boredom and more smartphone use incl. excessive use; screen time longer when dislike higher than usual. Essex repository record.
- Dora, van Hooff, Geurts, Kompier & Bijleveld 2021, Royal Society Open Science, doi 10.1098/rsos.201915 (PMC8261226): 83 PhD candidates, hourly ratings over 3 workdays, phone use logged; half-SD (~10 pts/100) more boredom → 1.43× as likely to use phone in next 20 min; not longer; boredom slightly higher after use (small); observational, exploratory, Android only.
- Wilson et al. 2014, Science 345:75–77 (PMC4330241): 11 studies, 6–15 min alone with thoughts typically not enjoyed; shock study: of those who said they'd pay to avoid the shock, 12 of 18 men and 6 of 24 women shocked themselves at least once in 15 min. Fox et al. 2014, Frontiers in Psychology (PMC4260464): most found thinking somewhat enjoyable; shock rated only slightly unpleasant; majority didn't shock.
- Mann & Cadman 2014, Creativity Research Journal 26(2):165–173: Study 1 n=80 (copying phone numbers), Study 2 n=90; boring tasks → more creative uses for cups; boring reading > writing; daydreaming proposed mechanism. Lab, short-term.
- Tagliaferri et al. 2025, Brain Sciences 15(8):794: systematic review, 28 studies; trait boredom linked to problematic tech use directly and indirectly; mostly cross-sectional, self-report, student samples.

Left out: the "bored to death" civil-servants study (often misreported), Camerini r values (only seen in a news summary), any "dopamine" mechanism claims, default-mode-network claims, the 30–40% figure in media coverage (used the paper's 1.43× instead).

## Product signal

Dora et al. found phone pickups after boredom were short (often under 2 minutes) and left boredom slightly higher; Tam et al. found the driver is how much you dislike boredom. Rep Rot's lock fits this (a set between the bored moment and the feed) and the post can say so honestly without new claims. Nothing here names a specific change to the core loop that passes the evidence test; boredom-tolerance coaching would be a new feature outside the loop.

Verdict: **none**.
