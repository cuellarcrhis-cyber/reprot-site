## Search intent

They sit for work and want to know what to actually do about it: how often
to get up, for how long, and which moves are worth it.

## Must-cover

- How often to get up (every 30–60 minutes) and for how long
- Does a workout cancel out sitting? (partly)
- A handful of desk-side moves: walk, chair squats, hip flexor stretch, incline push-ups on the desk, calf raises
- Standing desks: help or hype?
- Easier versions and a safety note

## Questions people ask

- How often should you get up from your desk? (Columbia trial: 5 min every
  30 min was the only dose that lowered both blood sugar and blood pressure;
  hourly breaks did less.)
- Does exercise cancel out sitting all day? (Ekelund 2020 BJSM harmonised
  meta-analysis, 44,370 people: ~30–40 min/day MVPA weakened the link between
  sitting and death risk; observational.)
- Are standing desks worth it? (Cochrane 2016: very low quality evidence they
  cut sitting in the short term; health effects unproven.)

## Our edge

1. One clear rule up front ("every 30 minutes, a few minutes on your feet")
   with the small trial behind it and its caveats.
2. Honest correction of the "40 minutes undoes 10 hours" headline.
3. A 3-minute desk-break menu with easier versions, plus a day plan that
   attaches breaks to things that already happen (calls, refills, the
   bathroom).
4. A take on standing desks that doesn't oversell them.

## Sources

- Duran AT, ... Diaz KM. "Breaking Up Prolonged Sitting to Improve
  Cardiometabolic Risk: Dose-Response Analysis of a Randomized Crossover
  Trial." Med Sci Sports Exerc 2023;55(5):847–855. doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000003109.
  11 adults, 8 h of sitting, five walking schedules vs none; 5 min every 30
  min was the only schedule that significantly lowered both glucose and BP;
  all schedules lowered BP ~4–5 mmHg; most lowered fatigue and improved mood;
  no change in cognition. Small, acute lab trial.
- Columbia University Irving Medical Center press release via ScienceDaily,
  12 Jan 2023.
- Diaz KM et al. "Patterns of Sedentary Behavior and Mortality in U.S.
  Middle-Aged and Older Adults." Ann Intern Med 2017;167(7):465–475.
  7,985 adults 45+, hip accelerometer; both more total sitting and longer
  unbroken bouts linked to higher mortality; highest risk with both.
  Observational.
- Ekelund U et al. "Joint associations of accelerometer-measured physical
  activity and sedentary time with all-cause mortality: a harmonised
  meta-analysis in more than 44 000 middle-aged and older individuals." Br J
  Sports Med 2020;54:1499–1506. 9 cohorts, 44,370 people, 4–14.5 years;
  ~30–40 min/day MVPA attenuated the sitting–mortality link. Observational.
- Dunstan DW et al. "Breaking up prolonged sitting reduces postprandial
  glucose and insulin responses." Diabetes Care 2012;35(5):976–983. 19
  overweight/obese adults 45–65, crossover; 2-min light or moderate walking
  breaks lowered post-drink glucose and insulin vs uninterrupted sitting.
- Shrestha N et al. "Workplace interventions for reducing sitting at work."
  Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2016, CD010912.pub3; Cochrane news summary.
  Very low quality evidence that sit-stand desks cut sitting at work short
  term; health effects unproven.
- WHO guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour (2020): limit
  sedentary time, replace with activity of any intensity.

## Product signal

The research's practical rule is about *frequency* (break up sitting every
30 minutes), and the app's triggers are an app limit and a wake-up alarm, not
a clock. For a desk worker, an app limit on the app they drift to during work
does produce a movement break, but only when they drift; it isn't a sitting
timer. A "move every 30 minutes" interval trigger would fail test 1 (it's a
new trigger type and a new use case, not the core move-to-unlock loop) and
test 2 (one small 11-person acute trial). The post is honest that Rep Rot
isn't a sitting timer. Verdict: **none**.
